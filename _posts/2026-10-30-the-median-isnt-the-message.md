During my undergraduate studies, I came across an essay that has stayed with me for years — “The Median Isn’t the Message” by the evolutionary biologist Stephen Jay Gould (1985).

In 1982, Gould was diagnosed with abdominal mesothelioma, a rare and aggressive form of cancer. After undergoing surgery, he asked his doctor for medical literature about his condition. She gently discouraged him from reading it, but being a scientist, Gould went straight to the Harvard medical library. What he discovered was devastating: the median survival time for patients with his cancer was just eight months.

But Gould understood that the median was not an expiration date. It meant that half the patients survived less than eight months, while the other half survived longer. And some survived *much* longer. The distribution had a *long right tail*, with survival times extending years beyond the median. He also recognized that his relatively young age, early diagnosis, and access to excellent medical care might place him among those with better prospects. The eight-month statistic described a population, not his individual fate.

Gould ultimately lived another twenty years, dying in 2002 from a different cancer. It's tempting to see his survival as a triumphant validation of his reasoning. But that would miss something essential about his argument. **His reasoning would have been just as valid had he died nine months after his diagnosis.**

A probability is not a promise, and the fact that an unlikely outcome occurs doesn't mean the original probability was wrong. Gould's essay has an even deeper observation: we tend to regard statistical averages as the meaningful reality and the variation around them as incidental noise. But Gould argued that we have it backward. The variation is the reality. The averages and medians are merely abstractions we construct to make sense of it.

Such abstractions are extraordinarily useful. They allow us to summarize enormous amounts of data so that we can make decisions. But there's a price to that simplification: a single number can conceal a great deal of complexity and diversity of outcomes, including rare ones with enormous consequences.

I've been thinking about this increasingly since becoming a father. Yes, statistics help us understand a complicated world, but a single statistic can only tell us so much. A number that might have once seemed sufficient for making a decision can suddenly feel lacking when the consequences concern my family. A risk can be “statistically negligible” and still represent something unimaginably consequential. An outcome that barely affects the median can change a life forever. And just like that — suddenly, the tails of the distribution matter enormously.

However, there's a bit of a paradox hiding here. On one hand, the more aware I become of the risks hidden in the tails, the stronger my instinct is to protect my children from them. To keep them safe and comfortable by "eliminating risk" (a ridiculously futile effort that would even put Sisyphus to shame). On the other hand, I don't want my desire to protect them to prevent them from experiencing life.

I want my children to explore, discover, take chances, make mistakes, and grow. To stand with one foot in order, and the other in chaos (to paraphrase Jordan Peterson), to encounter challenges, develop confidence, and gradually find their own way in the world. This makes uncertainty and risk unavoidable. So part of the challenge of parenthood is learning how to gradually introduce our children to the full “distribution of life”.

When they're young, we inevitably filter much of the world for them. We choose which experiences they're ready for, which risks are reasonable, and how much difficulty they can handle. We help them make sense of disappointment and frustration without overwhelming them. But, as they grow, that filter should gradually become less restrictive. They should encounter a wider range of experiences, assume greater responsibility, and learn to navigate situations that we can no longer (and perhaps should no longer) control for them.

Now, as a father of two, I return to Gould's essay with a very different appreciation compared to when I was an undergrad. Back then, I was fascinated by its statistical insight, but today, I’m more focused on what that insight means for the people I love. The median is useful precisely because it simplifies. But life is lived in the variance, not the mean.
