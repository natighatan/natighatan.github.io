A couple of days ago, I was discussing entropy with someone, and it reminded me of one of my favorite stories from the history of science.

During the 19th century, physics was grappling with a puzzle born from the smoke and steel of the Industrial Revolution. Engineers knew that no steam engine could ever be perfectly efficient - some energy always escaped as unusable heat. Rudolf Clausius formalized this phenomenon through a new quantity he called entropy, and together with the second law of thermodynamics, the equations described nature remarkably well.

But they left behind a deeper question: Why does heat spontaneously flow from hot objects to cold ones? Why does entropy almost always increase? The mathematics worked, but the underlying mechanism wasn’t understood.

One possible explanation that already gained traction was that maybe heat was nothing more than the collective motion of atoms. But many influential physicists remained unconvinced that atoms were real physical objects rather than merely useful - but fictional - mathematical concepts. Could the laws of thermodynamics really emerge from the statistical behavior of invisible particles?

Among the strongest defenders of that idea was the Austrian physicist Ludwig Boltzmann. Boltzmann believed there was no need for a new kind of physics. He argued that the laws of thermodynamics emerged naturally from the collective behavior of atoms. His revolutionary insight was to use probability to connect the microscopic molecular world with the macroscopic laws of heat.

He proposed that entropy is related to the number of microscopic arrangements - or microstates - that correspond to the same observable state of a system. The more microscopic configurations that produce the same macroscopic state, the higher the entropy. This was a radical shift in thinking. Instead of treating the second law of thermodynamics as an absolute law, Boltzmann argued that it’s statistical. Systems evolve toward high-entropy states not because nature somehow “prefers” them, but because there are overwhelmingly more ways for them to exist.

Today this idea is widely accepted, of course, but at the time it was deeply controversial.

One of Boltzmann’s most vocal critics was the physicist and philosopher Ernst Mach - the same physicist after whom the Mach number is named. Mach argued that science should concern itself only with things that can, at least in principle, be observed. Since atoms were invisible, he regarded them as speculative metaphysical constructs rather than legitimate scientific entities.

Sadly, the relentless criticism took a heavy toll on Boltzmann. Coupled with what is now believed to have been severe bipolar disorder, it led to recurring episodes of depression. In September 1906, while vacationing with his family in Duino, Italy, Boltzmann took his own life at the age of 62.

The tragedy is that he died just as the evidence supporting his ideas was becoming overwhelming. The year before, in 1905, an obscure patent clerk named Albert Einstein published a paper explaining Brownian motion - the seemingly random jittering of tiny pollen grains suspended in water.

Einstein proposed that the pollen grains weren’t moving randomly on their own. Instead, they were constantly being bombarded from all directions by countless invisible water molecules. Using Boltzmann’s statistical mechanics, he derived precise mathematical predictions for how far the grains should wander over time.

When experiments later confirmed Einstein’s predictions, they provided some of the strongest evidence yet that atoms were real. Boltzmann had been right.

Years later, when his remains were moved to Vienna’s Central Cemetery (Zentralfriedhof), the scientific community honored him in a fittingly elegant way. Engraved above his bust is one of the most famous equations in all of physics:

S = k ln W

where S is entropy, k is Boltzmann’s constant, and W (from the German Wahrscheinlichkeit, meaning “probability”) is the number of microscopic configurations consistent with the system’s observable state.

Imagine a gas initially confined to one side of a box. There are relatively few microscopic arrangements that keep every molecule crowded into half the box, but an unimaginably larger number in which the molecules are spread throughout the entire volume. The gas doesn’t expand because nature “likes disorder.” It expands because almost every possible microscopic arrangement looks like an expanded gas. Probability - not preference - drives the process.

Perhaps the greatest irony of all is that Boltzmann never actually wrote this famous equation. In his own papers, he developed the underlying mathematics through detailed analyses of molecular motion and combinatorics. He described the relationship between entropy and probability, but never expressed it in the concise form now engraved on his monument.

That final step was taken by Max Planck in 1900. While developing his theory of blackbody radiation, Planck wrote the equation in its modern form and introduced the constant we now call Boltzmann’s constant. Planck wrote the equation, Boltzmann conceived the idea.

Boltzmann’s insight transformed entropy from a mysterious thermodynamic quantity into a statement about probability. With that connection he didn’t just explain why heat flows the way it does - he changed how physicists think about the relationship between microscopic randomness and macroscopic laws.

And in one of the most beautiful examples of ideas transcending their original field, Boltzmann’s statistical view of entropy - a framework developed to explain steam engines - would later inspire Claude Shannon’s information theory. But that’s perhaps a subject for another post
